## 4. John Rawls (1921–2002), *A Theory of Justice* (1971)
Readings: *The Case for Equality* (written by Michael Sandel, from his book *Justice*; examples like Harvard students and "my sons" are Sandel's) + Chapman's review (1975)

**The problem:** most of us **never signed a social contract**. So why obey the law?
- **Locke:** **tacit consent**, which is a "pale form of the real thing."
- **Kant:** **hypothetical consent**: a law is just if the whole public *could* have agreed to it.
- **Rawls:** a **hypothetical agreement in an initial situation of equality**.

**Thought experiment**
- **Original position:** we choose principles of justice together.
- **Veil of ignorance:** we don't know our class, gender, race, religion, talents or health.
  → no one has **bargaining power** → whatever we agree to is **fair** (**justice as fairness**).
- A hypothetical contract is not a weaker version of a real one. It is a **pure form** of a real contract, so it is *morally stronger*.

**Moral limits of actual contracts:** real contracts get their force from **autonomy** (voluntary) + **reciprocity** (mutual benefit). Both are imperfect in real life.
- **Consent is not *enough*:** baseball cards traded between brothers; the $50,000 leaky toilet.
- **Consent is not *necessary*:** Hume's house repair; the squeegee men.
- **Consent vs benefit confused:** Sam's Mobile Repair van.

**What we'd choose behind the veil**
- ❌ **Utilitarianism:** you might end up in the oppressed minority ("Christian thrown to the lions").
- ❌ **Laissez-faire / libertarian:** you might end up homeless, not Bill Gates.
- ✅ **Two principles of justice:**
  1. **Equal basic liberties** for all (speech, religion, conscience). These have **priority** over social utility.
  2. **Difference principle:** social and economic inequalities are allowed **only if they benefit the least advantaged**.
- Chapman: the principles are **lexically ordered**: (1) greatest equal freedom → (2) **fair equality of opportunity** → (3) difference principle, which must **maximise** the prospects of the least advantaged (**maximin**).

**Argument from moral arbitrariness:** four theories of distributive justice

| Theory | Based on | Rawls's verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Feudal / caste | Accident of **birth** | Arbitrary |
| Libertarian | Free market + **formal** equality of opportunity | Arbitrary (social and economic starting points) |
| Meritocratic | Free market + **fair** equality of opportunity | Still arbitrary (the **natural lottery** of talents) |
| **Egalitarian** | **Difference principle** | ✅ Only one that isn't arbitrary |

- **Harrison Bergeron** (Vonnegut): the levelling nightmare. Rawls doesn't handicap the talented. He treats **natural talents as a common asset** and shares the rewards.
- **Objection 1, incentives:** inequality is OK *only if* incentives help the least well off.
- **Objection 2, effort:** effort itself depends on family and upbringing (the birth-order poll).
- **Rejects moral desert:** justice isn't about rewarding virtue. Instead: **entitlements to legitimate expectations** (they arise *after* the rules of the game are set).
  - **Lottery** (chance): entitled, but didn't deserve it.
  - **Game of skill:** entitlement and desert can differ.
  - What society values is also arbitrary (fresco painters vs programmers; Letterman vs a teacher; Woody Allen's *Stardust Memories*).
- **vs Milton Friedman ("Life is not fair"):** Rawls: "The natural distribution is neither just nor unjust… What is just and unjust is **the way institutions deal with these facts**." We agree **"to share one another's fate."**
- Focus = the **basic structure of society**, not individual salaries.

**Chapman's review: key points**
- Rawls draws on **Hume** (circumstances of justice), **Kant** (the self, a **noumenal self**) and **Rousseau** (moral psychology). Mill: **"accidents of birth"** are arbitrary.
- Rawls = the culmination of adapting **Rousseau's general will** to the modern state. Ends with: "Once again a **'Legislator'** has appeared."
- Like Rousseau, he has two stages: (1) rational choice; (2) **stability**, because our moral psychology supports it. We are **"naturally good"** and **"perfectible."**
- Three phases of moral development: **original position → institutional → familial**. **Reciprocity** is "a deep psychological fact."
- **Priority of the right over the good**: the **plurality of persons** rules out utilitarian trade-offs.
- **Reflective equilibrium:** testing principles and intuitions against each other until they fit.
- **Aristotelian Principle:** people enjoy using complex capacities. **Social union of social unions**; **noncomparing groups** keep envy down.
- **Primary goods**, including **self-respect**.
- Institutions: **constitutional democracy + market economy** (capitalist or socialist). Meade's **property-owning democracy**.
- **Critics:**
  - **Nozick:** **historical-entitlement** theory, possessive individualist, "Lockean." Says the difference principle invades **property in one's person**.
  - **Hayek:** the "mirage" of social justice.
  - **Macpherson:** still "possessive individualism."
  - **Equalitarians:** unequal *worth* of liberty.
  - Labels he's been given: Christian, Hobbist, socialist, utilitarian…
- **Gap:** the veil constrains self-interest only in theory. Once lifted, politics is **"regulated rivalry."**
- Rawls's theory = "the **high water mark of liberalism**."

## 5. Michel Foucault, "Panopticism" from *Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison* (1975)

**1. Plague town (end of the 17th century):** a model of **disciplinary mechanism**
- **Strict spatial partitioning:** town closed, quarters under **intendants**, streets under **syndics**, people locked indoors.
- **Inspection functions ceaselessly**: "the gaze is alert everywhere." Daily roll call at windows: "the great review of the living and the dead."
- **Permanent registration**: everything is written down and passed from the centre to the periphery.
- Power is **capillary** and reaches the smallest details of everyday life. Each person gets their "true" name, place, body and disease.
- The **political dream of the plague** = a **disciplined society**, the opposite of the literary "festival" of the plague.

**2. Leper vs plague:** two models of power

| Leper | Plague |
|---|---|
| **Exclusion**, exile-enclosure | **Discipline**, inclusion and segmentation |
| **Binary division** (pure / impure) | **Multiple separations**, individualising distribution |
| Marked / branded | **Analysed and distributed** |
| The "great Confinement" | "Correct training" |
| Dream of a **pure community** | Dream of a **disciplined society** |

- In the 19th century the two combine: **treat "lepers" as "plague victims"**. Binary branding (**mad/sane, normal/abnormal**) is applied *through* disciplinary techniques in the asylum, penitentiary, reformatory, school and hospital.
- "Jurists imagined the **state of nature** → rulers dreamt of the **state of plague**." (A direct link to Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau!)

**3. Bentham's Panopticon**
- An **annular building** of cells around a **central tower**. **Backlighting** makes each inmate a "small captive shadow."
- Reverses the **dungeon** (enclose / deprive of light / hide): keeps only *enclose*. **"Visibility is a trap."**
- **Axial visibility** (seen by the tower) + **lateral invisibility** (can't see neighbours) → no plots, no copying, no contagion. The crowd becomes **separated individualities**.
- "He is seen, but he does not see. He is the **object of information**, never a **subject in communication**."
- **Major effect:** "a state of **conscious and permanent visibility** that assures the **automatic functioning of power**."
- Power must be **visible** (you see the tower) and **unverifiable** (you never know if you're being watched).
- It **dissociates the see/being-seen dyad**: the ring is seen without seeing; the tower sees without being seen.
- **Automatizes and disindividualizes power**: anyone can operate it, with any motive.
- **"He becomes the principle of his own subjection"** → power becomes **non-corporal**, a "perpetual victory."
- "A real subjection is born mechanically from a **fictitious relation**." No bars, no chains: a **"house of certainty"**.
- **Le Vaux's menagerie at Versailles** → the Panopticon is a "royal menagerie" with man instead of animals.
- A **laboratory of power**: experiments on people; **knowledge follows power**.
- **Polyvalent**: prisons, hospitals, schools, workshops, asylums. "Power of mind over mind."
- **Democratically controlled**: anyone can enter the tower ("the great tribunal committee of the world").
- **Diagram of a mechanism of power**, a **political technology**, not a dream building.

**4. Discipline vs sovereignty**
- **Sovereign power** = the body of the king: visible, violent, discontinuous ("right of the sword").
- **Disciplinary power** = continuous, subtle, everywhere, **productive** (increases production, education, morality).
- **"Political anatomy"**: its object is **relations of discipline**, not relations of sovereignty.
- End point: a **disciplinary society**, "penetrated through and through with disciplinary mechanisms."

## 6. Hannah Arendt: *Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil* (1963)
Reading: Stephen J. Whitfield, "Hannah Arendt and the Banality of Evil" (1981)

**Background:** Adolf **Eichmann**, SS lieutenant-colonel, organised the **transportation of Jews** to death camps. Captured by Israeli agents in **Argentina**, tried in **Jerusalem (1961)** inside a **glass booth**, hanged in **1962**. Arendt covered the trial as a reporter for the ***New Yorker***.

**Arendt's thesis: the banality of evil**
- Eichmann was **"terribly and terrifyingly normal"**: not a pervert, not a sadist, not demonic.
- **"Manifest shallowness"** in the doer. Evil couldn't be traced to deep roots or motives.
- He killed no one personally. For him killing was **impersonal and abstract**: following rules, **obeying orders**, arranging train schedules.
- He felt **no hatred** for the Jews and had no firm ideology. He called himself an "**idealist**" but believed in nothing. His only motive was **career advancement**.
- **Thoughtlessness**, not fanaticism. "Eichmann **never realized what he was doing**."
- **Conscience reversed:** it would have bothered him only if he had *disobeyed* orders.
- "Evil in the Third Reich had **lost the quality of temptation**."
- He was a **bureaucrat in a modern state** (the **R.S.H.A.**, Reich Security Head Office). **Bureaucracy as well as ideology** made him dangerous.
- "**Dutiful conscience**" and "official conscientiousness" *facilitated* genocide.

**Radical evil vs banal evil**
- *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951): **radical evil**, "hell established on earth" under Hitler and Stalin.
- *Eichmann in Jerusalem*: evil can also be **banal**. **Gershom Scholem** pointed out the shift and called "banality of evil" a mere **"catchword."**
- Arendt did **not** mean that banality is itself evil, that all evil is banal, or that anyone could have done it.

**Other side (the prosecution, Gideon Hausner):** Eichmann as a "demonic," "perverted personality." Psychological tests (**Szondi**, **Selzer**'s Bender-Gestalt / House-Person-Tree) claimed violence. Whitfield: the tests are "much too pat." Eichmann was **law-abiding** and a **stable family man**.

**Supporting evidence and comparisons**
- **Milgram experiment:** about 2/3 of ordinary people gave maximum shocks out of **"a sense of obligation"** (obedience to authority) → supports banality of evil.
- **Freud:** "The state… desires to **monopolize** wrong-doing." Totalitarian killers were "**clerks scrupulous in their obedience**," not rampaging Cossacks.
- *In Cold Blood* (Capote): similar lack of remorse, but Eichmann wasn't a psychopath "given an SS uniform."
- **Elie Wiesel:** "if he were sane, I should choose madness." **Thomas Merton:** the sane may be "the most dangerous."
- Unlike literary villains (Milton, Goethe, Richard III), Eichmann was **motiveless, affectless, shallow**.
- **Criticism:** Arendt took Eichmann's own account too readily (his zeal against **Hungarian Jews** went beyond orders). She was accused of seeming to take his side, but she **did not excuse him**.
- **Lesson:** this kind of destructiveness is still "a threat **to everyone**."

## Comparison Table

| | **Rawls** | **Foucault** | **Arendt** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Key work | *A Theory of Justice* (1971) | *Discipline and Punish* (1975) | *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963) |
| Central question | What is a **just** society? | How does **modern power** work? | How do ordinary people commit **great evil**? |
| Key concept | Justice as fairness, veil of ignorance | Panopticism, disciplinary power | Banality of evil |
| View of the individual | Rational, free and equal moral person | Produced and **normalised** by discipline | Thoughtless functionary / responsible agent |
| Power | Should be organised by fair principles | Everywhere, invisible, **self-imposed** | Exercised through **bureaucracy** and obedience |
| Link to the contract tradition | **Revives** it (hypothetical contract) | Contrasts the **state of nature** with the **state of plague** | Challenges **Hobbesian obedience** |
| Key image | Veil of ignorance | Tower, cells, backlighting | Man in the glass booth |
| Criticised by | Nozick, Hayek, Macpherson | (Bentham thought it would stay democratically controlled) | Scholem, Hausner, critics of her portrait |

### Links to Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau (for Section B)
- **Rawls ← Locke:** rejects **tacit consent**. Nozick defends Lockean **property in one's person**.
- **Rawls ← Rousseau:** **general will**, natural goodness, perfectibility, the **Legislator** (Chapman).
- **Rawls ← Hobbes:** original position = a hypothetical **state of nature**.
- **Foucault vs Hobbes:** visible **sovereign** ("body of the king") → invisible **discipline**.
- **Foucault & Rousseau:** chains are no longer needed. People become "the **principle of their own subjection**."
- **Arendt vs Hobbes:** absolute **obedience** to authority → Eichmann's crimes.
- **Arendt & Foucault:** the obedient, normalised functionary inside a bureaucratic, disciplinary system.
